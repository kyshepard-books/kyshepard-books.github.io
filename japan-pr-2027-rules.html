Japan Permanent Residency: The 2027 Rules, Explained in Plain English

If you live in Japan and permanent residency (PR) is part of your plan, the rules of the game changed in October 2026, and the new version applies from April 1, 2027. The ten-year requirement you already know has not moved. Almost everything around it has.

What actually changed

The fee. Applying costs ¥10,000 today. From April 2027 it costs ¥200,000. Combined with review times that routinely exceed a year, a refusal becomes one of the most expensive letters you will ever receive.

Japanese language. Applicants are now expected to show Japanese ability around B1 level: everyday conversation, reading and writing for daily life. Some routes are exempt, notably the highly skilled points route, which is one reason the points calculation matters more than it used to.

Household income. The question is no longer just "do you earn enough?" Your household income is compared with the average for Japanese households of the same size. Who counts as your household, including family you support abroad, changes the answer.

Your future pension. Examiners now look forward: based on your record, what pension will you receive in old age? A thin projection is a negative factor, and savings or investments may be part of how you answer it.

Time abroad. Continuous residence has harder edges. A single trip of six months or more, or total absences of two and a half years or more across your ten years, can break it. Employer-ordered business trips need documentation.

The three-year question. PR requires the longest period of stay available for your status. A three-year period of stay has long been treated as the longest in practice, but that treatment is time-limited around the changeover. If you hold three years, when you apply may matter as much as whether you qualify.

Cancellation. PR itself can be cancelled in more circumstances than before, including deliberate failure to pay taxes or social insurance. The status is becoming conditional in a way it was not.

Why people are refused under the current rules, too

While researching refusal cases in the foreign community, I kept meeting the same stories. The man who paid all his tax but kept no receipts for the period when it was not deducted from his salary, and could not prove he had paid on time. The applicant whose company went bankrupt during the review. The engineer with more than 80 points who was refused anyway. The man who simply forgot to report a job change within 14 days.

None of these people failed the headline requirements. They failed the audit layer: records, timing, and reports.

A ten-minute self-audit

Before you file, sit down with documents, not memory:

  1. Pension record, read month by month online. Any gaps, especially around job changes?
  2. Five years of resident tax certificates. Any self-paid period without receipts?
  3. Every employer listed, and every change reported to immigration within 14 days?
  4. Days outside Japan in ten years, counted. Any single trip near six months?
  5. Household income against the Japanese average for your household size.
  6. If you hold a three-year period of stay, your application date checked against April 1, 2027.
  7. A guarantor lined up, and the document list taken from the Immigration Services Agency's current page.

Any "not sure" is a finding. Fix it before you apply. Under the old rules a refusal cost you a year. Under the new ones it costs a year and ¥200,000.
